Milestone 5 plan: re-dispatch

November 10, 2026

In milestone 4, dispatch only planned when someone called POST /plans, and the plan was greedy. Milestone 5 makes dispatch react on its own. A new or changed order, a truck that breaks down, a plant going offline or a site running late each trigger a re-dispatch. A re-dispatch freezes the loads that are already batching, repairs only the part of the plan the event touched, and improves the result with a time-boxed local search. It stores the outcome as a new plan version, with a diff that gives a reason for every change. Benchmarks measure all of this at 50, 200 and 1,000 loads, and make demo plays the whole story end to end.

Done when:

  • In make up, dispatch re-plans by itself on every trigger in SPEC §4: a new order, a volume change, a cancellation, a truck out of service, a plant offline, a site delay. Each re-dispatch that changes the plan becomes a new version with a diff.
  • Loads with a delivery, meaning batching has started, never move. Property tests check this together with every hard constraint.
  • Local search lowers the weighted soft objectives of the greedy plan within its 500 ms budget, and never breaks a hard constraint.
  • make demo seeds orders through the customer webhooks, plays the plant for one load, breaks a truck down, delays a site by 30 minutes and prints the plan diffs.
  • criterion benchmarks run at 50, 200 and 1,000 loads, and the README shows the results.
  • make test, make audit and CI are green.

Where re-dispatch sits

What triggers a re-dispatch

SPEC §4 triggerWhere it comes fromWhich loads it affects
NewOrderOrder intake: an order_upserted for an unknown orderThe new order
OrderVolumeChangedOrder intake: an order_upserted that changes the volume. Changes to start, pour rate, site or mix trigger it as well, with their own reason.That order’s loads that aren’t frozen
OrderCancelledOrder intake: order_cancelledThat order’s loads that aren’t frozen. They are removed.

PlantOfflineREST: PUT /plants/{id}/status. Telematics has no plant status, and the plant-agent can report it in M7.Every order with a load at that plant that isn’t frozen

Three more cases are always repaired as well, whatever the trigger: orders that weren’t in the previous plan yet, orders that were unplanned, and loads whose planned batch time has passed without batching. POST /plans is a full re-plan: every load that isn’t frozen counts as affected.

How a re-dispatch repairs the plan

  1. Freeze. Every load with a delivery stays exactly as it is. A delivery exists once the plant has reported batching for that load. Frozen loads keep their trucks and bays busy for the rest of the run. An order’s frozen volume counts towards its total, and its new loads continue the sequence numbers.
  2. Keep. Loads of orders the triggers didn’t touch stay where they were in the previous version.
  3. Construct. The M4 heuristic plans the affected orders around the frozen and kept loads. Trucks now start from their last reported GPS position instead of their home plant.
  4. Improve. Local search runs for up to 500 ms over every load that isn’t frozen. A churn penalty keeps it from reshuffling the fleet over one late pump.
  5. Verify. The independent checker from M4 runs on the result. If it finds a violation, dispatch falls back to a full re-plan. If that fails too, it keeps the previous version and logs an error. An invalid plan is never stored.
  6. Diff and store. If anything changed, the plan is stored as the next version with its triggers and its diff. A re-dispatch that changes nothing stores nothing.

Every change gets a reason. If a trigger touched the load, the reason names the trigger. If the load only follows an earlier load of its order, the reason says so. If local search moved it to make room, the reason names the order it made room for.

Local search

The construction heuristic places one load at a time and never looks back. Local search fixes what that misses, using the two moves SPEC §4 names:

  • Relocate. Move one load to another truck or plant, then re-time the rest of its order so the pour stays continuous.
  • Swap. Exchange the trucks of two loads that batch within an hour of each other, then re-time both orders.

Re-timing reuses the heuristic’s slot search, so every candidate meets the hard constraints by construction. A move that can’t place a whole order is rejected. A move is kept only if it lowers the weighted cost. Moves are random, with a seeded RNG, until the budget runs out. At the end, orders that are still unplanned get one more try, because the moves may have freed capacity.

Cost termDefault weightWhy
Minute beyond an order’s maximum gap20Cold joints: the heaviest weight, as SPEC §4 asks
Minute of any gap between loads1A continuous pour, even inside the limit
Minute the first load arrives late2Lateness against the requested start, or the delayed start after a site delay
Empty kilometre0.5Now measured on the finished plan, truck by truck
Load moved compared with the previous version10Churn, re-dispatch only. A load has moved when its truck, plant or batch time differs.

In other words, one minute over an order’s gap limit costs as much as 10 minutes of lateness, and moving a load costs as much as 5. Every weight is an environment variable. Tests stop after a fixed number of moves instead of after a time budget, so their results are reproducible.

Frozen loads and the hard constraints

A frozen load is a fact: the plant is batching it, or already has. Our model has no returns, so the load gets delivered, even if its truck reports a breakdown or its plant goes offline afterwards. The planner never decides anything about a frozen load. The hard constraints apply to what the planner decides:

CheckLoads the planner placedFrozen loads
Truck in service, plant onlineYesNo: they were placed while both were fine
Load ≤ drum, discharge within the mix limit, opening hoursYesNo: these are facts the plant reported, not decisions
One load per truck at a time, bays ≤ loading baysYesThey count as occupied time. A violation is reported whenever a placed load is involved.
Unchangedn/aYes: every frozen load must appear exactly as it went in

This is the one place where M5 needs your explicit OK (decision 1). CLAUDE.md says never to weaken a hard constraint, and I read the constraints as rules for the planner’s decisions. I don’t read them as a demand that the past should never have happened.

Plan versions and diffs

Each version now records the version it replaced, its triggers in words, and its diff. A diff lists added, removed and moved loads by order and sequence, with before and after values for truck, plant, batch time, ETA and volume. It also lists orders that became unplanned or planned, the reason for each change, and the metrics before and after. Each load also carries frozen, so readers can tell a fact from a plan.

Tying a batch ticket to a load

The plant’s ticket gets two optional fields, order and load_no, and BatchTicketIssued gets an optional load reference. A real batch computer knows the order it batches for, because dispatch sends it. In M7 that is the plant-agent’s job. A ticket with a reference creates or advances a delivery, and a ticket without one is ignored. The simulated plants keep sending tickets without a reference, because their trucks still make random trips.

make demo

The demo is a small binary in the simulators crate, so it can send orders in the customers’ own formats with the code that already renders them. It runs in a container, so make demo needs only Docker, like make up. It goes like this:

  1. Start a fresh stack with slower order generation, so the scenario stays readable. The demo restarts it with the normal settings at the end.
  2. Seed two orders through the real channels: a bauwerk-ag JSON webhook and a neckar-infra XML webhook. Both start in about 45 minutes, at different ship-tos. Wait until a plan version includes them, and print their loads.
  3. Play the plant for load #1 of the first order: publish batching, loading and loaded tickets with the load reference over MQTT. Wait until the order shows in_progress.
  4. Break down the truck that carries load #2. The demo publishes to sim/trucks/{id}/breakdown, and the simulated truck reports out_of_service through its normal flaky modem. Wait for the version this triggers, and print its diff.
  5. Delay the second order’s site by 30 minutes through the API, and print the diff.
  6. Print a summary: load #1 never moved, and every stored version passed the hard-constraint checker.

Diffs only list the demo orders in full. Knock-on changes to other orders are counted on one line.

Benchmarks

crates/dispatch/benches/planner.rs uses criterion at 50, 200 and 1,000 loads. The world is the seeded Rhine-Neckar one, with the fleet scaled to the load count (one truck per 8 loads) so the plans stay feasible, and with seeded random orders. It measures:

  • construct: the greedy plan from scratch;
  • improve: a fixed number of local search moves, reporting moves per second and how far the cost drops;
  • repair: one truck out of service on a full plan, end to end, with the 500 ms budget.

The target is a repair at 1,000 loads in under 1 s. If it misses, I’ll fix the hotspot M4 already flagged: the bay check rescans every interval. make bench runs the benchmarks, and the README gets a results table. CI compiles the benchmarks through clippy --all-targets but doesn’t run them, because shared runners are too noisy.

Steps

Each step ends green and becomes its own commit, as in M4.

Step 1

Frozen loads in the planner, as pure code. First, check rust-toolchain.toml and the crate versions. The planner then takes frozen loads, starts trucks from their last position, plans in-progress orders whatever their start, and uses delayed starts. The checker treats frozen loads as facts. Hand-made scenarios cover:

  • a pour frozen halfway;
  • a volume cut below what is already batched;
  • a cancellation with frozen loads;
  • a truck breaking down while it carries a frozen load.

Property tests freeze random prefixes of random plans.

Step 2

Cost and local search, as pure code. Tests check that:

  • the cost never goes up;
  • no hard constraint breaks on random worlds;
  • a fixed seed with a fixed number of moves always gives the same plan;
  • a hand-made case where the greedy plan leaves a cold-joint gap gets it closed by local search.

Step 3

Repair and diffs, as pure code. There is one scenario per trigger, plus the fallback to a full re-plan. Property tests apply a random trigger to a random plan and check that:

  • the plan stays valid;
  • frozen loads stay unchanged;
  • with local search off, orders the trigger didn’t touch keep exactly the loads they had;
  • every change has a reason.

Step 4

Benchmarks. Add the criterion benches and make bench, then optimise only what misses the 1 s target.

Step 5

Events into dispatch. This step adds:

  • the migration;
  • the load reference on tickets (domain, ingest and a sample);
  • the fleet consumer;
  • triggers from the order intake;
  • the worker, with versions that store triggers and diffs.

Integration tests use Postgres and NATS containers and check four cases:

  • a truck’s out_of_service event produces a version that moves that truck’s loads;
  • a ticket freezes its load;
  • a cancellation removes loads;
  • a stale truck status is ignored.

Step 6

REST API. Add plant status, site delays, diffs, the derived in_progress and the OpenAPI document. Router tests check each endpoint and run the checker on every version.

Step 7

Simulator breakdowns and make demo. Simulated trucks break down on command, and a test covers it. Then:

  • add the demo binary and its compose service and image to CI;
  • add make demo and make bench;
  • update the README section “Re-dispatch” and .env.example.

Finally, run make demo end to end on a fresh stack and commit.

Decisions I need from you

I’ll use the default unless you say otherwise. Decisions 1 to 3 are domain questions, and decision 5 changes something the M4 plan promised.

#QuestionMy default
1What happens to a frozen load whose truck breaks down or whose plant goes offline afterwards?It stays in the plan as a fact, and the checker exempts it from the per-load checks, as in the table above. The alternative is to drop the load and plan its volume again. That needs a “lost load” state, which our model deliberately doesn’t have.
2What does “site delayed 30 min” mean?It applies to the site’s orders that are in progress or start today, in Berlin time. Each gets a delayed start: the later of its start and now, plus 30 minutes. The delay survives customer revisions, and lateness is measured against the delayed start. The alternative is a delay per order.
3When is a broken truck back in service?When its telematics reports any other status. The simulated truck does that after TRUCK_REPAIR_MINUTES. Its return triggers no re-dispatch, and the next trigger uses it.
4How is a ticket tied to a load?Through the optional order and load number on the ticket. Tickets without them are ignored. Matching by truck, plant and time would guess wrong whenever the simulated trucks make their random trips.
5Do the simulated trucks follow the plan in M5?No, that moves to M7. The M4 plan said M5 would do it. But following the plan in real time would make the demo wait 20 minutes or more for batching. The plant-agent in M7 pulls the plan anyway, so plan-following belongs there. Until then, the demo plays the plant for its own loads.
6Does make demo reset the stack?Yes. It runs make down (deleting the local volumes) and then make up with slower order generation. Only then is the outcome predictable. On a stack that has run for hours, simulator orders have booked the fleet, and the demo orders may end up late or unplanned.
7What about an order whose start passed over an hour ago and that was never batched?It leaves the plan, and the diff says why. That keeps M4’s rule. Orders in progress are always planned.
8Are the cost weights right?The defaults are in the local search table. Churn counts loads whose truck, plant or batch time changed.
9Do deliveries follow the truck’s statuses after loaded (left_plant to unloaded)?Not yet. Freezing only needs batching, so orders reach in_progress but not shipped or delivered. Tying truck statuses to deliveries fits M7, once the trucks follow the plan.

Heads-up

  • In the live stack, plan and reality still diverge: the simulated trucks make random trips (decision 5). In the live stack, loads only freeze in the demo.
  • Local search is time-boxed, so the live service doesn’t reproduce a plan bit for bit. The tests do.
  • Re-dispatch latency, pour-gap distributions and traces of a trigger through to its diff are milestone 6. M5 only logs them.
